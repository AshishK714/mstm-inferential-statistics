Inferential statistics and hypothesis testing

Practice for the exam: test a claim, and estimate a population value, from one survey. Parts 6 and 7 review the midterm ideas and data visualization.

What the exam asks

You are given the results of one survey. You ask two questions of it.

  • Test. Someone has stated a value, called the claim. Is the survey consistent with the claim?
  • Estimate. Nobody has stated a value. What does the survey alone tell you about the population?

You answer both questions from the survey result and its standard error (SE).

Parts 6 and 7 add questions without calculations: the roles of variables, Simpson's paradox and survivorship bias from the midterm, and the types of variable, types of analysis and charts from data visualization.

Part 1 of 7 · questions 1 to 8

1Concepts

Read each topic, and then answer the question under it.

Population and sample

  • Population: the whole group you want to know about, for example all the adults in a city. Its size is N and its mean is written μ.
  • Sample: the n people you ask, because you cannot ask everyone.
  • Survey mean: the mean of the sample. You know it exactly.
  • You do not know μ. The survey mean is your estimate of μ.

Why the estimate is uncertain

The survey mean is not exactly equal to μ, for two reasons:

  • People differ.
  • The sample is only a part of the population. A different sample of the same size would give a different mean.

There would be no uncertainty in two cases:

  • Every person has the same value.
  • You survey the whole population.

Larger samples: the law of large numbers

  • The mean of a larger sample tends to be closer to the population mean μ.
  • Example: a mean from 400 people is usually closer to μ than a mean from 25 people.

Individual values and sample means

  • There are two distributions: the distribution of individual values, and the distribution of sample means.
  • The example uses a very small population, so that you can check every number by hand.

Example. A small café has 8 regular customers. Table 1 gives the amount that each customer spent on the last visit. A sample is 2 customers chosen at random.

1. The population

One row is one customer. 8 rows.

2. Twelve samples of 2 customers

In each sample, one row is one customer. 2 rows.

3. The sample means

One row is one sample. 12 rows.

The two distributions

One square is one data point: one customer under "Individual values", and one sample under "Sample means". The number above each column is the count.

What one data point is

  • Individual values (table 1): one data point is the spend of one customer. The mean of these values is μ = $5 and their standard deviation is σ = $2.
  • Sample means (table 3): one data point is the mean of one sample.

Sample size and number of samples

  • Sample size, n = 2: the number of customers in one sample. It is the number of rows in each of the 12 small tables.
  • Number of samples = 12: how many times a sample was taken. It is the number of rows in table 3.
  • Only n is used in the formulas. The number of samples is not used in any formula.
  • In a real survey you take one sample, for example one sample of n = 400 people. The other samples are imagined. You imagine them to ask how different your survey mean could have been.

Each of the 2 customers is chosen at random from all 8, so the same customer can be chosen twice. In a large population this almost never happens.

The mean of the sample means is μ

  • The 12 samples in the example are only some of the samples that could be taken.
  • There are 8 × 8 = 64 possible samples of 2 customers: 8 choices for the first customer and 8 for the second.
  • The table counts how many of the 64 samples have each mean. The chart is the distribution of the 64 sample means, under the distribution of the 8 customers.

The two distributions have the same center, $5. The sample means are less spread out than the customers.

The mean of each of the 64 samples

Rows: the first customer chosen. Columns: the second customer chosen. Each cell is the mean of that sample, in dollars.

  • The mean of all the sample means is exactly μ.
  • Reason: every customer is in the same number of samples. In the mean of all the sample means, every customer therefore counts equally, exactly as in μ.
  • One sample mean can be above μ or below μ. Sample means have no tendency to be too high or too low. For this reason the survey mean is used as the estimate of μ.

The standard deviation of the sample means is the standard error

  • The standard deviation of the sample means has its own name: the standard error (SE).
  • The table gives the result for every possible sample of 1, 2 and 4 of the 8 customers.
  • The standard deviation calculated from the list of sample means is equal to σ / √n.
  • Sample means differ less than customers do. In a sample, a customer with a high value is usually averaged with customers with lower values.
  • Example: a sample has a mean of $9 only if every customer in it is customer C. This is 1 of the 8 samples of 1 customer, and only 1 of the 4,096 samples of 4 customers.
  • A sample that is 4 times as large has half the standard error: $2 for n = 1 and $1 for n = 4.
  • In a survey you do not know σ. You use the standard deviation of your sample, s, so SE = s / √n.

Sample means are normal: the central limit theorem

  • When the sample is large enough, the means of samples are approximately normally distributed.
  • This is true even when the population is not normal, for example when most people spend nothing and a few people spend a lot.

Simulation. The population:

  • 75% of people spend nothing, and the others spend different amounts.
  • The mean is $2 and the standard deviation is $4.
  • The population is strongly skewed.

Choose the sample size n. The number of samples is always 1,000. The chart shows the 1,000 sample means, so one data point in the chart is the mean of one sample.

The height of a bar is the number of sample means at that value. The dashed line is the population mean, $2. The shaded band is 2 standard errors on each side of it.

The empirical rule

In a normal distribution:

  • about 68% of values are within 1 standard deviation of the mean
  • about 95% are within 2 standard deviations
  • about 99.7% are within 3 standard deviations

For sample means:

  • Sample means are approximately normal.
  • Their standard deviation is the standard error.
  • The rule therefore applies to sample means, with the SE as the unit: about 95% of sample means are within 2 SE of μ.

Example. The population mean is $30 and the standard error is $1. The chart is the distribution of sample means.

Standard deviation and standard error

  • Standard deviation (SD): describes people. It measures how far one person usually is from the mean.
  • Standard error (SE): describes sample means. It measures how far the mean of a sample usually is from μ.
  • SE = SD / √n, so the SE is smaller than the SD.

A proportion is a mean

  • Write 1 for each person who says yes and 0 for each person who says no.
  • The mean of that column is the proportion p.
  • Its standard deviation is √( p × (1 − p) ).
  • Write proportions as decimals: 20% is 0.20.

2Standard error

What you need to know

  • The standard error (SE) is the standard deviation of sample means.
  • It measures how far a sample mean usually is from μ.
  • A small SE means that the survey mean is a precise estimate.
Standard error of a meanSE = s / √n
  • s is the standard deviation in your survey, and n is the sample size.
  • More difference among people gives a larger SE.
  • A larger sample gives a smaller SE.
Standard error of a proportionSE = √( p × (1 − p) / n )
  • A proportion is a mean: write 1 for yes and 0 for no, and the mean of that column is p.
  • The standard deviation of that column is √( p × (1 − p) ).

Worked example: SE of a mean

A bookstore surveys its customers. The standard deviation of the spend per visit is $10. The table gives the standard error for a survey of 100 customers and for a survey of 400 customers.

Worked example: SE of a proportion

In the survey of 100 customers, 20 customers are members of the loyalty program.

  2. SD of the 1s and 0s = √( 0.20 × 0.80 ) = √0.16 = 0.40

3A mean: test and estimate

What you need to know

Test, when a claim has been stated:

  • Assume for now that the claim is right.
  • Then 95% of sample means are within 2 SE of the claim. Calculate that range.
  • Survey mean inside the range: the survey is consistent with the claim.
  • Survey mean outside the range: a result such as yours would occur in fewer than 5% of samples, so the survey is not consistent with the claim.

Estimate, when there is no claim:

  • Set the claim aside.
  • The 95% interval is the survey mean ± 2 SE.

Connect:

  • The claim is inside the interval exactly when the survey mean is inside the range.
  • The two answers always agree, because both compare the same distance with the same 2 SE.

Worked example 1: the survey is not consistent with the claim

The head office of a bookstore chain states that customers spend $30 per visit on average. A store manager surveys 100 randomly chosen customers. The survey mean is $33 and the standard deviation is $10.

Test the claim

  1. SE = s / √n = 10 / √100 = $1
  2. If the claim is right, 95% of sample means are between 30 − 2 × 1 = $28 and 30 + 2 × 1 = $32.
  3. The survey mean is $33. Its distance from the claim is $3, which is 3 / 1 = 3 SE.
  4. $33 is outside the range $28 to $32.
  5. If the claim were right, fewer than 5% of samples would be this far from $30.
  6. Decision: the survey is not consistent with the claim of $30.

The curve is the distribution of sample means if the claim is right. The shaded part contains 95% of them. The open circle is the claim, and the filled circle is the survey mean.

Estimate

  1. SE = $1, the same as in the test.
  2. 95% interval = 33 ± 2 × 1 = $31 to $35
  3. Sentence: with about 95% confidence, the average spend per visit of all customers is between $31 and $35.

The shaded band is the 95% interval. The survey mean is in the middle of it.

Connect

  • Test: the survey mean, $33, is outside the range $28 to $32.
  • Estimate: the claim, $30, is outside the interval $31 to $35.
  • Compare: both answers follow from one comparison. The distance between $33 and $30 is $3, and that is more than 2 SE = $2.

Worked example 2: the survey is consistent with the claim

A second store of the same chain surveys 100 customers. The survey mean is $31.50 and the standard deviation is $10. The claim is the same: $30.

Test the claim

  1. SE = 10 / √100 = $1
  2. If the claim is right, 95% of sample means are between $28 and $32.
  3. The survey mean is $31.50. Its distance from the claim is $1.50, which is 1.5 SE.
  4. $31.50 is inside the range $28 to $32.
  5. If the claim were right, more than 5% of samples would be this far from $30. A result such as this one is common.
  6. Decision: the survey is consistent with the claim of $30.

Estimate

  1. 95% interval = 31.50 ± 2 × 1 = $29.50 to $33.50
  2. Sentence: with about 95% confidence, the average spend per visit of all customers of this store is between $29.50 and $33.50.

Connect

  • Test: the survey mean, $31.50, is inside the range $28 to $32.
  • Estimate: the claim, $30, is inside the interval $29.50 to $33.50.
  • Compare: the distance is $1.50, and that is less than 2 SE = $2.

"Consistent" does not mean that the claim is proved. A claim of $31 or of $33 would also be consistent with this survey, because both values are inside the interval.

4A proportion: test and estimate

What you need to know

The steps are the same as for a mean. Only the standard error is different:

  • Test: you assume that the claim is right, so the SE uses the claimed proportion.
  • Estimate: there is no claim to assume, so the SE uses the survey proportion. Round this SE to two decimals.
  • Write proportions as decimals: 20% is 0.20.

Worked example

The head office also states that 50% of customers buy a coffee. In the survey of 100 customers, 62 bought a coffee.

Test the claim

  1. Survey proportion = 62 / 100 = 0.62
  2. SE with the claimed proportion = √( 0.50 × 0.50 / 100 ) = √0.0025 = 0.05
  3. If the claim is right, 95% of sample proportions are between 0.50 − 2 × 0.05 = 0.40 and 0.50 + 2 × 0.05 = 0.60.
  4. The distance from the claim is 0.62 − 0.50 = 0.12, which is 0.12 / 0.05 = 2.4 SE.
  5. 0.62 is outside the range 0.40 to 0.60, so fewer than 5% of samples would be this far from 0.50.
  6. Decision: the survey is not consistent with the claim of 50%.

Estimate

  1. SE with the survey proportion = √( 0.62 × 0.38 / 100 ) = √0.002356 = 0.0485, which is 0.05 to two decimals.
  2. 95% interval = 0.62 ± 2 × 0.05 = 0.52 to 0.72
  3. Sentence: with about 95% confidence, between 52% and 72% of all customers buy a coffee.

Connect

  • Test: the survey proportion, 0.62, is outside the range 0.40 to 0.60.
  • Estimate: the claim, 0.50, is outside the interval 0.52 to 0.72.
  • Compare: the distance between 0.62 and 0.50 is 0.12, and that is more than 2 SE = 0.10.
  • The test used 0.50 in the SE and the estimate used 0.62. Both standard errors are 0.05 after rounding, so the conclusion is the same.

5Comparing surveys and stating conclusions

What you need to know

Two surveys can both be above the claim and still lead to different decisions:

  • It does not matter whether a survey is above or below the claim. When the claim is right, about half of all surveys are above it and about half are below it.
  • The decision depends on the distance in standard errors: more than 2 SE from the claim, or not.

The same survey mean can lead to different decisions when the sample sizes are different:

  • A larger sample has a smaller standard error.
  • The distance from the claim is then more standard errors, although the distance in dollars is the same.
  • A small difference from the claim is evidence against the claim only when the sample is large enough.

State your conclusions carefully:

  • A test does not give the chance that the claim is right.
  • A test tells you how often a survey such as yours would occur if the claim were right.
  • "Consistent with the claim" does not mean that the claim is proved. It means that the survey gives no reason to reject it.

  • With 25 or with 100 customers, $31.50 is less than 2 SE from the claim. The survey is consistent with the claim. This does not prove the claim. The sample is too small to decide.

The example used for the roles. A job-training programme recruits adults, trains them, arranges mentors and mock interviews, and helps them find work. It reports to the funders who pay for it.

Roles of variables

Question 29

Name the five roles a variable can play in a decision. Define each role and give an example.

Exam answer

RoleWhat it isExample in the job-training programme
Activity (input)The work the organisation does. It is the lever the organisation can pull, more or less of it.Running mock-interview workshops
ContextWhat shapes the result but cannot be changed within the period the plan covers.Regional unemployment; whether a participant has childcare
MechanismWhat has to happen between the activity and the result, often inside a person, for the activity to work.The participant gains confidence and skill in interviews

Detailed explanation

The chain. An organisation does something (the activity). That changes something in people or in the world (the mechanism). The change produces the result the organisation exists for (the outcome). Context sits around the whole chain and shapes every step, but the organisation cannot move it in the time it is planning for. The signal is an early reading taken along the way, because the outcome comes too late to steer by.

A second example: a coffee chain with a loyalty app.

  • Activity: sending discount offers through the app. The chain decides how many, to whom, and how large.
  • Context: the weather, a new competitor opening across the street, the neighbourhood a store is in. They affect sales, and the chain cannot change them this year.
  • Mechanism: the customer notices the offer, visits, and forms a habit of coming back. If customers ignore the offers, nothing else in the chain can work.
  • Signal: the share of customers who open the offers, and app sign-ups. They arrive within days.
  • Outcome: repeat visits and profit over the year. They are what the chain actually wants.

Why sort variables at all. The role tells you what to do with the variable. You act on activities, watch signals, work around context, check that the mechanism is happening, and judge success by the outcome. Question 32 takes this further.

A condition for learning anything. Analytics is the study of variation and co-variation. To learn what a lever does, the lever must vary and the outcome must vary. If every participant received exactly the same training, the data cannot tell you what the training does.

Common mistakes.

  • Calling a signal an outcome. Course completion is not what the programme exists for. It stands in for a job.
  • Treating context as a lever. A programme cannot lower regional unemployment, so a plan that depends on it is not a plan.
  • Confusing a mechanism with a signal. A mechanism is the change that produces the result. A signal is a measurement you watch. The mechanism is often not recorded in the data at all.

Question 30

How do you decide whether a variable is a signal or an outcome? Why does the difference matter for a decision?

Exam answer

Ask one question: would I still want this if it led to nothing else? If yes, it is an outcome, wanted for its own sake. If no, it is a signal: it stands in for an outcome that arrives later.

The difference matters because a signal can be pushed up while the outcome does not improve. A course completion rate can be raised by making the course easier, and no more graduates find jobs. So a signal should be watched as an early warning, and not made a target on its own.

Detailed explanation

Why signals exist. The outcome usually arrives too late to steer by. A job a year after graduation tells you how last year's course went, after the next course has started. A signal arrives early enough to act on: finishing rates, attendance, employer feedback.

Examples of signals. Employer feedback on graduates is a signal: the programme does not exist to collect good feedback, but good feedback is an early sign that job offers will follow. The number of employers offering interviews is a signal too, and it shows the danger: it can be raised by approaching more employers of lower quality.

The same problem in a business.

  • A website counts clicks (signal) because it wants sales (outcome). Clicks can be raised with misleading headlines that bring no sales.
  • A call centre counts calls answered within one minute (signal) because it wants customers' problems solved (outcome). Agents can answer quickly and rush customers off the phone.

This is often summed up as: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

How to use it in advice. When you report a signal, name the outcome it stands in for, and say what could push the signal up without the outcome improving. Where you can, pair a signal with a later check on the outcome, for example completion now and employment a year later.

Close calls. Some items depend on who is asking. Community trust in the programme is a signal to a funder (people who trust the programme enrol) and can be an outcome to the programme itself (an organisation that serves a community and is not trusted by it has failed at something it cares about). Asking the question is what matters.

Question 31

Is the role of a variable a fixed property of that variable? Explain with an example.

Exam answer

No. The role belongs to the pairing of a variable with a decision, so it depends on who is deciding and what they are deciding. Regional employment is context for the training programme, because the programme cannot change it. It is the outcome for a regional government, which is judged on it.

Even within one file the job of a column can change. When you report average attendance, attendance is the thing being measured. When you compare job placement for high and low attenders, attendance is the thing you group by.

Detailed explanation

More examples of one variable in two roles.

  • Price is an activity (a lever) for the seller who sets it, and context for the buyer who has to accept it.
  • The interest rate is context for a firm deciding whether to borrow, and an activity for the central bank that sets it.
  • Participant satisfaction is a signal to the person running the programme, and closer to an outcome for the participant who feels it.

Inside a table. The column that is measured is called a fact, and the column you slice by is called a dimension. In "average attendance was 93%", attendance is the fact. In "placement was higher for high attenders", attendance is the dimension and placement is the fact. The values of attendance did not change between the two questions. The question changed, and with it the job the column does.

How to use it. Before any analysis, write down the decision and the decision-maker. Then label the role of each variable for that decision. If three people make three decisions from one file, the same columns may need three sets of labels. A label copied from someone else's decision can lead you to try to pull a lever that is not yours.

Question 32

How do the five roles guide a decision, or the advice you give a manager?

Exam answer

Each role tells you what to do with the variable.

  • Activities: act on them. They are the only levers.
  • Signals: watch them for early warning, but do not reward them on their own.
  • Context: work around it. Compare like with like, and do not blame or credit the organisation for it.
  • Mechanism: check that it actually happens. An activity that changes nothing in people will not change the outcome.
  • Outcome: judge success by it.
Detailed explanation

A worked piece of advice. The programme's job placement rate fell this year, and the board asks whether to cut the mock-interview workshops. Use the roles to structure the answer.

  1. Context first. Did regional unemployment rise? If it did, placements may have fallen whatever the programme did. Compare with similar programmes or regions in the same year before blaming the workshops.
  2. The activity. Did the workshops actually change? Were there fewer of them, or did fewer people attend?
  3. The mechanism. Did participants who attended gain confidence and skill? If people attended but did not change, the problem is in how the workshops are run, not in how many there are.
  4. The signals. Did completion or employer feedback fall earlier in the year? If they did, the warning was there and the process for acting on signals needs fixing.
  5. The outcome. Judge the decision by placements in good jobs, not by a signal that can be pushed up.

Advice in one line: do not cut a lever because an outcome fell until you have ruled out context and checked whether the mechanism broke.

Why this structure helps. It separates what the manager can change (activities), what the manager should watch (signals), what the manager should check (mechanism), and what the manager should not be judged on (context). Mixing them up leads to two common errors: rewarding a team for good luck in its context, and punishing it for bad luck.

Simpson's paradox

Question 33

What is Simpson's paradox? Give an example.

Exam answer

Simpson's paradox happens when a comparison between groups reverses when the data are split by a third variable. One group is better within every subgroup, and yet it looks worse overall.

Example: two support teams are compared on the share of tickets resolved on time. Overall, Team A looks better. But Team B does better on simple tickets, and also does better on complex tickets. Team B looks worse overall only because most of its tickets are complex, and complex tickets are harder for everyone.

Detailed explanation

How the reversal comes about. The overall rate for a team is a blend of its rate on simple tickets and its rate on complex tickets. The blend depends on how many of each kind the team handled. Team A handled mostly simple tickets, which are usually resolved on time, so its blend is pulled up. Team B handled mostly complex tickets, which are often late, so its blend is pulled down. The overall comparison is mostly comparing the mix of tickets, not the teams.

Two more examples.

  • Hospitals. A large hospital has a lower overall survival rate than a small one. But the large hospital receives most of the seriously ill patients. Among mildly ill patients it does better, and among seriously ill patients it also does better.
  • Trainers. One trainer has the highest overall job placement rate. Another trainer does better with graduates and also better with school leavers, but was given mostly school leavers, who are placed less often whoever trains them.

The two ingredients. A reversal needs both:

  1. a third variable that has a strong effect on the outcome (ticket difficulty, how ill the patient is, education level), and
  2. groups that have very different mixes of that variable.

If either is missing, the overall comparison and the within-group comparisons agree.

Question 34

Why does Simpson's paradox happen?

Exam answer

An overall rate is an average of the subgroup rates, weighted by how many cases the group has in each subgroup. When a third variable strongly affects the outcome, and the groups have very different mixes of it, the overall rate measures the mix as much as the group itself. The group that has more of the hard cases looks worse overall, even if it is better in every subgroup.

Detailed explanation

An everyday version. Two students take the same courses. One takes mostly advanced courses, where grades are lower for everyone. The other takes mostly introductory courses. The first student can earn a higher grade than the second in the advanced courses, and a higher grade in the introductory courses, and still have a lower overall average. The overall average rewards the easy mix.

The name for the third variable. A variable that is related both to which group a case is in and to the outcome is called a confounding variable (or lurking variable). In the support-team example, ticket difficulty decides which team gets a ticket and also decides how likely it is to be resolved on time.

What it is not. Simpson's paradox is not an error in the arithmetic. Both the overall figures and the subgroup figures are correct. They answer different questions. The overall figure answers "which team resolved more of its own tickets on time?". The subgroup figures answer "which team does better on the same kind of ticket?". For judging the teams, the second question is usually the one that matters.

Question 35

How should Simpson's paradox change a decision, or the advice you give?

Exam answer

Before ranking, rewarding or choosing on an overall rate, ask whether a third variable decides which cases go to each group and also affects the outcome. If it does, compare the groups within each level of that variable, like with like, and base the decision on those comparisons.

Hold a variable still only if it was fixed before the groups acted, for example ticket difficulty set by a triage desk. Do not split by a label the groups chose themselves.

Detailed explanation

Steps to follow.

  1. List the variables that strongly affect the outcome (difficulty, severity, customer type, region).
  2. Check whether the groups have different mixes of them.
  3. If they do, compare the groups within each level.
  4. Report both the overall comparison and the within-group comparison, and explain why they differ.

Advice that changes.

  • Do not give Team A the bonus, or move complex tickets away from Team B, on the overall rate. Team B is better at both kinds of ticket.
  • Do not send patients to the small hospital because its overall survival rate is higher. For a patient with a given illness, the large hospital does better.
  • A marketing campaign that targeted hard-to-win customers may look worse overall than one that targeted loyal customers. Compare the two within each customer segment before choosing.

A limit. A reversal shows that the third variable matters. It does not prove that splitting by it gives the true answer, and there is no rule for when to stop adding variables. The argument has to come from the situation. If a triage desk set the difficulty before the teams saw the ticket, holding difficulty still compares like with like. If each team labelled its own tickets, a team could label its slow tickets "complex", and splitting by the label would hide part of what you are trying to measure.

Survivorship bias

Question 36

What is survivorship bias? Give an example.

Exam answer

Survivorship bias is drawing a conclusion only from the cases that made it through some process, when the cases that did not make it are missing from the data and differ from the ones that remain.

Example: to learn what makes a company succeed, you study only successful companies, and find that their founders took big risks. Many failed companies took the same risks, but they are not in the data, so risk-taking looks like a cause of success.

Detailed explanation

The course example. A software firm has two support teams. Team B's customers first go through a self-service portal that closes the easy questions. Only the harder contacts become tickets. Team A has no portal, so its tickets include easy and hard contacts. Comparing the two teams on their tickets compares all of Team A's contacts with only the hard ones of Team B. The easy cases that were resolved earlier never became rows in the file.

The classic example. In the Second World War, analysts looked at the bomber planes that came back from missions and marked where they had bullet holes, mostly on the wings and the body. The first idea was to add armour where the holes were. The statistician Abraham Wald pointed out that the planes that were hit in the engines did not come back, so they were not in the data. The armour belonged where the returning planes had no holes.

Everyday examples.

  • A satisfaction survey of current customers misses the customers who were unhappy and left.
  • The average return of investment funds that exist today looks high, because the funds that did badly were closed and dropped from the list.
  • "Old buildings were built better" ignores the old buildings that were badly built and have already fallen down.

Question 37

When does removing cases before the data are collected bias a conclusion, and when does it not?

Exam answer

It biases the conclusion when which cases were removed depends on the thing being measured, so the cases that remain differ from the ones removed. It does not bias the conclusion when cases are removed at random, because the remaining cases still look like the removed ones. The data are only smaller.

A portal that closed every third contact at random would not bias the team comparison. A portal that closes the easy contacts does.

Detailed explanation

Survivorship and selection. Both are filters that decide who or what gets into the file, and in both the filter is related to the thing being measured.

  • Survivorship takes cases out: the easy contacts closed by the portal, the companies that failed, the planes that did not return.
  • Selection lets unusual cases in. In the midterm, rural participants had to travel to a city centre to attend, so the rural people in the file were the most motivated and best supported. If a rural centre removes the travel, the new rural learners will include the people the travel kept out, and their results will be closer to the average for rural people in general.

In both cases the file describes a narrower group than its name suggests.

The question to ask. Was there a step before this file where some cases were resolved, dropped or turned away, and were those cases like the ones that remain?

Question 38

How should survivorship bias change a decision, or the advice you give?

Exam answer

Before drawing a conclusion, ask what a case had to go through to appear in the data, and who is missing. Then either collect data on the missing cases (the failures, the customers who left, the contacts closed earlier), or limit the conclusion to the group that made it through. Do not copy the habits of the survivors, or predict for a wider group, without looking at the cases that did not make it.

Detailed explanation

Advice that changes.

  • Before advising a client to copy the strategy of the ten most successful firms, look for firms that used the same strategy and failed. If many did, the strategy is not what made the winners win.
  • Before concluding that customers are satisfied, survey the customers who cancelled, for example with an exit survey.
  • Before judging Team B on its tickets, get the full list of contacts, including the ones the portal closed, or compare only tickets of the same difficulty.
  • Before opening a rural centre and promising the results of the current rural learners, expect lower results, because the new learners will include people the travel used to keep out.

Wording a conclusion honestly. If the missing cases cannot be found, say what the data cover: "Among customers who stayed for a year, satisfaction is high", not "Our customers are satisfied".

The other side of a filter. A filter can also be useful evidence. Someone who finished a demanding degree has shown motivation, because the degree was costly to get. Being in a filtered file tells you something about each person in it, even though the file does not represent the wider group.

7Variables, types of analysis and charts

This part reviews the types of variable, the types of analysis, and how to choose a chart. As in part 6, each question shows an exam answer, and Detailed explanation gives the reasoning and more examples.

Variables

Question 39

What is a variable? Give examples.

Exam answer

A variable is a characteristic that can be measured and that can take different values. The key idea is that it varies: between individuals (people differ in age) or within one individual over time (a person's weekly spending).

Examples: age in years; the number of courses taken this term; ethnicity; skill level in a sport.

Detailed explanation

Rows and columns. In a data table, each row is one individual (a person, a store, a ticket) and each column is one variable.

Why "varies" matters. A characteristic that is the same for everyone is not useful for analysis. If every student in a class took four courses, the number of courses cannot explain why some students got higher grades than others. Analytics studies variation in one variable and co-variation between variables.

The type of a variable decides what you can do with it: which summary is meaningful (a mean or a count) and which chart to draw. Questions 40 and 41 cover the types.

Question 40

What is the difference between a numeric and a categorical variable?

Exam answer

A numeric variable has values that are quantities, measured or counted, so arithmetic such as the mean is meaningful. Examples: age, tuition, number of courses.

A categorical variable has values that are labels that place each case in a group, so you count how many cases fall in each group. Examples: major, public or private university, skill level.

A quick test: does the average of the values mean something? If yes, the variable is numeric.

Detailed explanation

Numbers that are really labels. A variable written in numbers is not always numeric. A zip code, a student ID, or a code of 1 for rural and 2 for urban are categorical. The average zip code means nothing, and adding the codes for rural and urban counts nothing.

Why it matters.

  • For a numeric variable you can report the mean, median and standard deviation, and draw a histogram or a box plot.
  • For a categorical variable you report counts or percentages, and draw a bar plot.

Using the wrong summary gives a number with no meaning, for example "the average major is 2.4".

Question 41

Exam answer

Three questions that classify any variable.

  1. Does the average mean something? Yes: numeric. No: categorical.
  2. If numeric: is there always a possible value between two values? Yes: continuous (between 19 and 20 years old there is 19.5). No: discrete (nobody takes 4.5 courses).

Money and rounding. Money is counted in cents, but it has so many possible values that it is treated as continuous. Age recorded in whole years is still a continuous variable, recorded in steps.

Types of analysis

Question 42

What are univariate, bivariate and multivariate analysis? Give the summary used in each case.

Exam answer

  • Univariate: the pattern, or distribution, of one variable. Numeric: mean, median, mode and standard deviation. Categorical: counts or percentages in each category.
  • Bivariate: the relationship between two variables. Numeric and numeric: correlation. Numeric and categorical: the mean (or median, or standard deviation) of the numeric variable for each category, as in a pivot table. Categorical and categorical: counts for each combination of categories, in a cross table.
  • Multivariate: the relationship among more than two variables, for example whether the relationship between two variables is the same across the categories of a third.
Detailed explanation

Why multivariate matters. A relationship between two variables can change, or even reverse, once a third variable is taken into account. That is Simpson's paradox (question 33). Looking at a third variable is how you check whether a bivariate pattern is real or is produced by the mix of cases.

Mean or median. For a skewed variable such as income, the median is often a better summary of the typical value than the mean, because a few very large values pull the mean up (question 44).

Charts

Question 43

Which charts show the distribution of one variable? Which one do you use for a numeric variable, and which for a categorical one?

Exam answer

A numeric variable: a histogram, which shows the shape of the distribution, or a box plot, which shows the median, quartiles and outliers.

A categorical variable: a bar plot, with one bar per category and the height of each bar equal to the count or percentage in that category. For example, a bar plot of majors shows the most popular major in a course.

Detailed explanation

Why not a bar plot for a numeric variable? A numeric variable can have hundreds of different values, so one bar per value is unreadable. A histogram groups the values into ranges (bins) first, and then counts how many fall in each range.

Histogram or box plot? Use a histogram to see the shape: one peak or two, symmetric or skewed. Use a box plot to read the median and quartiles directly, to spot outliers, and to compare several groups side by side. The two answer slightly different questions, and it is common to draw both.

Bar plot tips. Sort the bars by height when the categories have no order (majors), so the largest is easy to see. Keep the natural order when the categories are ordinal (low, medium, high).

Question 44

What does a histogram show? Describe a normal, a right-skewed and a left-skewed distribution.

Exam answer

A histogram divides the values of a numeric variable into ranges (bins) and draws a bar for each bin. The height of the bar is the number of observations in that bin. It shows the shape of the distribution: where the values are concentrated, how spread out they are, and whether the shape is symmetric.

  • Normal: a symmetric bell shape. The mean and the median are about equal.
  • Right (positive) skew: a long tail to the right, from a few very large values. The mean is above the median. Example: income.
  • Left (negative) skew: a long tail to the left, from a few very small values. The mean is below the median. Example: scores on an easy exam.
Detailed explanation

How to remember the direction. The skew is named after the side of the tail, not the side of the peak. In a right-skewed distribution most values are on the left, and a few stretch far out to the right.

Why the mean moves. The mean uses every value, so a few very large values pull it towards the tail. The median is the middle value, so it does not move much. That is why reports of income and house prices usually give the median.

Why the shape matters for a decision. If a manager is told that the average customer spends $40, but spending is strongly right-skewed, most customers spend less than $40 and a few spend a great deal. A promotion designed for a "$40 customer" fits almost nobody. Look at the histogram before choosing which summary to report.

Outliers in a histogram. A histogram does not mark outliers. They appear as very short bars far from the rest of the data.

Question 45

What does a box plot show? Define Q1, the median, Q3, the IQR and an outlier.

Exam answer

A box plot summarises a numeric variable. The box runs from Q1 (the 25th percentile) to Q3 (the 75th percentile), and the line inside the box is the median (Q2, the 50th percentile). The whiskers run to the smallest and largest values that are not outliers.

  • IQR (interquartile range) = Q3 − Q1. It is the spread of the middle half of the data.
  • An outlier is a value below Q1 − 1.5 × IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR. It is drawn as a separate point.
Detailed explanation

A small example. Suppose Q1 = 20 and Q3 = 40. Then IQR = 40 − 20 = 20, and 1.5 × IQR = 30. Values below 20 − 30 = −10 or above 40 + 30 = 70 are outliers.

Reading the box. A quarter of the values are below Q1, a quarter between Q1 and the median, a quarter between the median and Q3, and a quarter above Q3. If the median line is close to Q1 and the upper whisker is long, the data are right-skewed.

The median, not the mean. The line inside the box is the median, not the mean. Some software adds a separate marker for the mean.

Where box plots are most useful. Side-by-side box plots compare a numeric variable across categories, for example tuition at public and at private universities. You see at once which group is higher, which is more spread out, and which has outliers.

Question 46

Which chart shows the relationship between two variables? Answer for each combination of variable types, for a trend over time, and for more than two variables.

Exam answer

  • Two numeric variables: a scatter plot, one dot per case. An upward slope shows a positive relationship, a downward slope a negative one, and no slope no relationship. The pattern may be straight (linear) or curved.
  • Numeric and categorical: a bar plot with one bar per category, the height being the mean or total of the numeric variable. To compare whole distributions, use side-by-side box plots.
  • Two categorical variables: a cross table of counts, or a grouped bar plot.
  • A trend over time: a line chart, with time on the x-axis, for example a share price.
  • More than two variables: add the extra variable to the chart with color, shape or size, for example a scatter plot colored by public or private.
Detailed explanation

Reading a scatter plot. Look for four things: the direction (up or down), the form (straight or curved), the strength (how tightly the dots follow the pattern), and any unusual points far from the rest.

Color for categories, size for numbers. A categorical third variable is shown with color or shape (public in one color, private in another). A numeric third variable can be shown with the size of the dot or with a gradient of color (larger dots for larger enrolment).

A relationship is not a cause. A scatter plot can show that two variables move together. It does not show that one causes the other. A third variable may drive both, which is why multivariate charts are useful.

Question 48

Why should you look at a chart, and not only report a summary number such as a correlation or a mean?

Exam answer

A single number can hide the shape of the data. A correlation measures only how close the points come to a straight line, so a correlation near zero fits several different pictures: no relationship at all, or a strong relationship that rises and then falls. A mean can hide skew and outliers. A chart shows the shape, so you see curves, outliers and groups before you choose a summary. Look at the scatter plot before computing a correlation, and at the histogram before reporting a mean.

Detailed explanation

A well-known demonstration. The statistician Francis Anscombe made four small data sets with the same means, the same standard deviations and the same correlation. Drawn as scatter plots, they look completely different: one is a straight-line trend, one is a curve, one is a straight line with one outlier, and one is a vertical stack of points with a single far-away point. The numbers alone could not tell them apart.

An example of a curve. Sales rise with the discount up to a point, and then fall because a deep discount makes customers doubt the quality. The correlation between discount and sales could be close to zero, and a manager reading only that number would conclude that discounts make no difference. The scatter plot shows that there is a best discount.

Advice. Draw first, then summarise. The chart tells you which summary is honest: a correlation if the pattern is a straight line, a median if the data are skewed, separate summaries if the data contain distinct groups.

The same thinking in your own decisions

  • Nothing in this part is on the exam. There is nothing to calculate and nothing to memorize.
  • In parts 3 to 5 you tested a claim from a report. People use the same thinking whenever someone wants to change what is currently believed.
  • Each heading below is a question. Think about your own answer first, and then open it.
What do people believe before any data are collected?
  • In every test in this guide there was a claim, for example "customers spend $30 per visit". You assumed that the claim was right, and you asked whether the survey was consistent with it.
  • The belief that is accepted until there is evidence against it is called the null hypothesis. It is the established belief.
  • Nobody needs data to keep the null hypothesis. It is accepted by default.
  • People collect data when they want to find evidence against it.
  • The method in one sentence: we collect evidence to test whether we can reject the null hypothesis.
  • If the evidence is not strong enough, the null hypothesis is kept. This does not prove it. In parts 3 to 5 this was the decision "the survey is consistent with the claim".
Who has to bring the evidence?

The person who wants to reject the established belief has to bring the evidence. This is called the burden of proof.

  • The null hypothesis depends on the situation. In a criminal trial the accused does not have to prove anything. In a loan application the applicant has to prove that they can repay.
  • A question to think about: the founder has already built and sold two companies and has a degree from Stanford or Harvard. Does the investor now start from a different belief? Or is the belief the same, and the founder's record is strong evidence against it? Both descriptions are reasonable. In both, the investor needs less new evidence from this founder than from a first-time founder.

You are deciding whether to pursue another degree. You do not know what the job market will be when you finish. There are two actions and two possible futures.

  • You can be wrong in two ways, and you cannot make both errors rare with the same data. If you ask for stronger evidence before you reject a belief, you reject true beliefs less often and you keep false beliefs more often.
  • You therefore have to decide which error is worse for you, and how often you are willing to make it.
  • For the degree: which of the two errors would you regret more?
  • Statistics books call the two errors the Type I error (rejecting a true belief) and the Type II error (keeping a false belief).
Why is the rule 5%, and not 20%?
  • The rule in this guide was 2 standard errors. With that rule, if the established belief is in fact true, you reject it wrongly only 5% of the time.
  • In business conversation people say this as "I am 95% confident". The note at the end of this page gives the exact meaning.
  • The rule is strict because a wrong rejection of an established belief is costly. Other people have made plans that depend on the belief. The person who rejected it, and was wrong, loses money or reputation.
  • The percentage is a choice. It depends on how strongly the belief is held and on the cost of rejecting it wrongly. 5% is usual in business questions. 1% or less is used when a wrong rejection would be very costly.
  • Courts in the United States make the same choice in words. A criminal conviction requires proof "beyond a reasonable doubt", because convicting an innocent person is very costly. A civil case requires only that the claim is "more likely than not".
  • Think of one belief in your company that you would reject only with very strong evidence, and one that you would reject with little evidence. What is the difference between them?
Is it unfair that a new idea needs proof and an old idea needs none?
  • It can seem unfair. A new idea needs strong evidence, and the established idea is kept without any.
  • Stability is useful. If a little evidence were enough to reject any belief, a company would change its strategy after every bad week, and nobody could plan.
  • No belief should be protected from questions either. Anyone may challenge an established belief.
  • Evidence allows both: the belief stays until someone brings enough data against it.
  • There is a price. With a stricter rule, a wrong belief is kept for longer.
Two people see the same data. Why do they still disagree?

A store manager is evaluated on customer satisfaction. The target is that at least 80% of customers are happy. A survey of 400 customers finds that 79% are happy.

  • For 400 customers, SE = √( 0.80 × 0.20 / 400 ) = 0.02, so 2 SE is 4 percentage points.
  • The null hypothesis is the opposite of what the person who brings the data wants to show.

More data. With more customers the standard error is smaller, and a small difference becomes enough evidence.

The exact statement is: if the established belief is in fact true, this rule makes you reject it wrongly only 5% of the time. Technical discussion: the exact percentage in a test

  • This discussion is not on the exam. On the exam you compare the distance with 2 standard errors, and you write "fewer than 5%" or "more than 5%".
  • Statistics books and software report an exact percentage in place of "fewer than 5%". Read this discussion if you want to know how that percentage is calculated and how a decision is made from it.
  • The examples are the bookstores of part 3. The head office claims that customers spend $30 per visit on average. Each store surveys 100 customers and finds a standard deviation of $10, so SE = $1.
  • The first store found a survey mean of $33 and the second store found $31.50. A third store is added here. Its survey mean is $32.17.
What are the two ways to test a claim?

First way: start from the survey.

  • Calculate the 95% interval: survey mean ± 2 SE.
  • Check whether the claimed μ is inside the interval.
  • If the claimed μ is outside the interval, reject the claim.

Second way: start from the claim.

  • Assume that the claim is right. Then the mean of all possible sample means is the claimed μ, and their standard deviation is the SE. Part 1 showed both facts with the café of 8 customers.
  • Calculate how many SE the survey mean is from the claimed μ.
  • From that number, calculate how often a sample mean would be that far from μ, or farther.
  • If that would happen in fewer than 5% of samples, reject the claim.

How often would a sample mean be this far from the claim?
  • Sample means are approximately normal, so the percentage depends only on the distance in SE.
  • "This far" counts both directions: sample means this far above the claimed μ, and sample means this far below it.

The percentage is 3%. What does it mean, and what do you decide?

The third store found a survey mean of $32.17. That is 2.17 SE from the claim of $30, and the percentage is 3%.

  • If the claim is right, only 3% of all possible samples have a mean this far from $30, or farther.
  • Suppose you say: "This sample does not come from a population with a mean of $30." If the claim is in fact right, you are wrong. You would be wrong for 3% of all possible samples. These are the samples with means this far from $30.
  • People say this as: "We reject the claim with 97% confidence."
  • The requirement is 95% confidence. It allows a wrong rejection for 5% of samples. 3% is less than 5%.
  • Decision: reject the claim. In other words, reject the null hypothesis.

The rule for any test:

  • The percentage is less than 5%: reject the claim.
  • The percentage is more than 5%: keep the claim. This does not prove the claim.
  • 5% is a choice. The question "Why is the rule 5%, and not 20%?" on this page explains it. If the requirement is 99% confidence, the percentage has to be less than 1%.

The first way agrees, whatever the confidence:

  • An interval with more confidence is wider. The 95% interval is the survey mean ± 2 SE, the 97% interval is ± 2.17 SE, and the 99% interval is ± 2.58 SE.
  • Third store, 95% interval: 32.17 ± 2 × 1 = $30.17 to $34.17. The claim, $30, is outside it, so the claim is rejected.
  • Third store, 97% interval: 32.17 ± 2.17 × 1 = $30.00 to $34.34. The claim is exactly at the end of it.
  • Third store, 99% interval: 32.17 ± 2.58 × 1 = $29.59 to $34.75. The claim is inside it, so the claim is kept.
  • "Reject with 97% confidence" therefore has an exact meaning: the claim is outside every interval that has less than 97% confidence.

What the 3% is not. The 3% is not the chance that the claim is right, and 97% is not the chance that your decision is right. The 3% is calculated on the assumption that the claim is right. It is the share of samples that would be this far from the claim. Question 27 in part 5 asks you to correct this mistake.
