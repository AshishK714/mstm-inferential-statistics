Inferential statistics and hypothesis testing

Practice for the exam: test a claim, and estimate a population value, from one survey.

What the exam asks

You are given the results of one survey. You ask two questions of it.

  • Test. Someone has stated a value, called the claim. Is the survey consistent with the claim?
  • Estimate. Nobody has stated a value. What does the survey alone tell you about the population?

You answer both questions from the survey result and its standard error (SE).

Part 1 of 5 · questions 1 to 8

1Concepts

Read each topic, and then answer the question under it.

Population and sample

  • Population: the whole group you want to know about, for example all the adults in a city. Its size is N and its mean is written μ.
  • Sample: the n people you ask, because you cannot ask everyone.
  • Survey mean: the mean of the sample. You know it exactly.
  • You do not know μ. The survey mean is your estimate of μ.

Why the estimate is uncertain

The survey mean is not exactly equal to μ, for two reasons:

  • People differ.
  • The sample is only a part of the population. A different sample of the same size would give a different mean.

There would be no uncertainty in two cases:

  • Every person has the same value.
  • You survey the whole population.

Larger samples: the law of large numbers

  • The mean of a larger sample tends to be closer to the population mean μ.
  • Example: a mean from 400 people is usually closer to μ than a mean from 25 people.

Individual values and sample means

  • There are two distributions: the distribution of individual values, and the distribution of sample means.
  • The example uses a very small population, so that you can check every number by hand.

Example. A small café has 8 regular customers. Table 1 gives the amount that each customer spent on the last visit. A sample is 2 customers chosen at random.

One row is one customer. 8 rows.

2. Twelve samples of 2 customers

In each sample, one row is one customer. 2 rows.

3. The sample means

The two distributions

One square is one data point: one customer under "Individual values", and one sample under "Sample means". The number above each column is the count.

What one data point is

  • Individual values (table 1): one data point is the spend of one customer. The mean of these values is μ = $5 and their standard deviation is σ = $2.
  • Sample means (table 3): one data point is the mean of one sample.

Sample size and number of samples

  • Sample size, n = 2: the number of customers in one sample. It is the number of rows in each of the 12 small tables.
  • Number of samples = 12: how many times a sample was taken. It is the number of rows in table 3.
  • Only n is used in the formulas. The number of samples is not used in any formula.
  • In a real survey you take one sample, for example one sample of n = 400 people. The other samples are imagined. You imagine them to ask how different your survey mean could have been.

Each of the 2 customers is chosen at random from all 8, so the same customer can be chosen twice. In a large population this almost never happens.

The mean of the sample means is μ

  • The 12 samples in the example are only some of the samples that could be taken.
  • There are 8 × 8 = 64 possible samples of 2 customers: 8 choices for the first customer and 8 for the second.
  • The table counts how many of the 64 samples have each mean. The chart is the distribution of the 64 sample means, under the distribution of the 8 customers.

The two distributions have the same center, $5. The sample means are less spread out than the customers.

The mean of each of the 64 samples

Rows: the first customer chosen. Columns: the second customer chosen. Each cell is the mean of that sample, in dollars.

  • The mean of all the sample means is exactly μ.
  • Reason: every customer is in the same number of samples. In the mean of all the sample means, every customer therefore counts equally, exactly as in μ.
  • One sample mean can be above μ or below μ. Sample means have no tendency to be too high or too low. For this reason the survey mean is used as the estimate of μ.

The standard deviation of the sample means is the standard error

  • The standard deviation of the sample means has its own name: the standard error (SE).
  • The table gives the result for every possible sample of 1, 2 and 4 of the 8 customers.
  • The standard deviation calculated from the list of sample means is equal to σ / √n.
  • Sample means differ less than customers do. In a sample, a customer with a high value is usually averaged with customers with lower values.
  • Example: a sample has a mean of $9 only if every customer in it is customer C. This is 1 of the 8 samples of 1 customer, and only 1 of the 4,096 samples of 4 customers.
  • A sample that is 4 times as large has half the standard error: $2 for n = 1 and $1 for n = 4.
  • In a survey you do not know σ. You use the standard deviation of your sample, s, so SE = s / √n.

Sample means are normal: the central limit theorem

  • When the sample is large enough, the means of samples are approximately normally distributed.
  • This is true even when the population is not normal, for example when most people spend nothing and a few people spend a lot.

The empirical rule

In a normal distribution:

  • about 68% of values are within 1 standard deviation of the mean
  • about 95% are within 2 standard deviations
  • about 99.7% are within 3 standard deviations

For sample means:

  • Sample means are approximately normal.
  • Their standard deviation is the standard error.
  • The rule therefore applies to sample means, with the SE as the unit: about 95% of sample means are within 2 SE of μ.

Example. The population mean is $30 and the standard error is $1. The chart is the distribution of sample means.

Standard deviation and standard error

  • Standard deviation (SD): describes people. It measures how far one person usually is from the mean.
  • Standard error (SE): describes sample means. It measures how far the mean of a sample usually is from μ.
  • SE = SD / √n, so the SE is smaller than the SD.

A proportion is a mean

  • Write 1 for each person who says yes and 0 for each person who says no.
  • The mean of that column is the proportion p.
  • Its standard deviation is √( p × (1 − p) ).
  • Write proportions as decimals: 20% is 0.20.

2Standard error

What you need to know

  • The standard error (SE) is the standard deviation of sample means.
  • It measures how far a sample mean usually is from μ.
  • A small SE means that the survey mean is a precise estimate.
Standard error of a meanSE = s / √n
  • s is the standard deviation in your survey, and n is the sample size.
  • More difference among people gives a larger SE.
  • A larger sample gives a smaller SE.
Standard error of a proportionSE = √( p × (1 − p) / n )
  • A proportion is a mean: write 1 for yes and 0 for no, and the mean of that column is p.
  • The standard deviation of that column is √( p × (1 − p) ).

Worked example: SE of a mean

A bookstore surveys its customers. The standard deviation of the spend per visit is $10. The table gives the standard error for a survey of 100 customers and for a survey of 400 customers.

Worked example: SE of a proportion

In the survey of 100 customers, 20 customers are members of the loyalty program.

  1. p = 20 / 100 = 0.20
  2. SD of the 1s and 0s = √( 0.20 × 0.80 ) = √0.16 = 0.40
  3. SE = √( 0.20 × 0.80 / 100 ) = √0.0016 = 0.04

3A mean: test and estimate

What you need to know

Test, when a claim has been stated:

  • Assume for now that the claim is right.
  • Then 95% of sample means are within 2 SE of the claim. Calculate that range.
  • Survey mean inside the range: the survey is consistent with the claim.
  • Survey mean outside the range: a result such as yours would occur in fewer than 5% of samples, so the survey is not consistent with the claim.

Estimate, when there is no claim:

  • Set the claim aside.
  • The 95% interval is the survey mean ± 2 SE.

Connect:

  • The claim is inside the interval exactly when the survey mean is inside the range.
  • The two answers always agree, because both compare the same distance with the same 2 SE.

Worked example 1: the survey is not consistent with the claim

The head office of a bookstore chain states that customers spend $30 per visit on average. A store manager surveys 100 randomly chosen customers. The survey mean is $33 and the standard deviation is $10.

Test the claim

  1. SE = s / √n = 10 / √100 = $1
  2. If the claim is right, 95% of sample means are between 30 − 2 × 1 = $28 and 30 + 2 × 1 = $32.
  3. The survey mean is $33. Its distance from the claim is $3, which is 3 / 1 = 3 SE.
  4. $33 is outside the range $28 to $32.
  5. If the claim were right, fewer than 5% of samples would be this far from $30.
  6. Decision: the survey is not consistent with the claim of $30.

The curve is the distribution of sample means if the claim is right. The shaded part contains 95% of them. The open circle is the claim, and the filled circle is the survey mean.

Estimate

  1. SE = $1, the same as in the test.
  2. 95% interval = 33 ± 2 × 1 = $31 to $35
  3. Sentence: with about 95% confidence, the average spend per visit of all customers is between $31 and $35.

The shaded band is the 95% interval. The survey mean is in the middle of it.

Connect

  • Test: the survey mean, $33, is outside the range $28 to $32.
  • Estimate: the claim, $30, is outside the interval $31 to $35.
  • Compare: both answers follow from one comparison. The distance between $33 and $30 is $3, and that is more than 2 SE = $2.

Worked example 2: the survey is consistent with the claim

A second store of the same chain surveys 100 customers. The survey mean is $31.50 and the standard deviation is $10. The claim is the same: $30.

Test the claim

  1. SE = 10 / √100 = $1
  2. If the claim is right, 95% of sample means are between $28 and $32.
  3. The survey mean is $31.50. Its distance from the claim is $1.50, which is 1.5 SE.
  4. $31.50 is inside the range $28 to $32.
  5. If the claim were right, more than 5% of samples would be this far from $30. A result such as this one is common.
  6. Decision: the survey is consistent with the claim of $30.

Estimate

  1. 95% interval = 31.50 ± 2 × 1 = $29.50 to $33.50
  2. Sentence: with about 95% confidence, the average spend per visit of all customers of this store is between $29.50 and $33.50.

Connect

  • Test: the survey mean, $31.50, is inside the range $28 to $32.
  • Estimate: the claim, $30, is inside the interval $29.50 to $33.50.
  • Compare: the distance is $1.50, and that is less than 2 SE = $2.

"Consistent" does not mean that the claim is proved. A claim of $31 or of $33 would also be consistent with this survey, because both values are inside the interval.

4A proportion: test and estimate

What you need to know

The steps are the same as for a mean. Only the standard error is different:

  • Test: you assume that the claim is right, so the SE uses the claimed proportion.
  • Estimate: there is no claim to assume, so the SE uses the survey proportion. Round this SE to two decimals.
  • Write proportions as decimals: 20% is 0.20.

Worked example

The head office also states that 50% of customers buy a coffee. In the survey of 100 customers, 62 bought a coffee.

Test the claim

  1. Survey proportion = 62 / 100 = 0.62
  2. SE with the claimed proportion = √( 0.50 × 0.50 / 100 ) = √0.0025 = 0.05
  3. If the claim is right, 95% of sample proportions are between 0.50 − 2 × 0.05 = 0.40 and 0.50 + 2 × 0.05 = 0.60.
  4. The distance from the claim is 0.62 − 0.50 = 0.12, which is 0.12 / 0.05 = 2.4 SE.
  5. 0.62 is outside the range 0.40 to 0.60, so fewer than 5% of samples would be this far from 0.50.
  6. Decision: the survey is not consistent with the claim of 50%.

Estimate

  1. SE with the survey proportion = √( 0.62 × 0.38 / 100 ) = √0.002356 = 0.0485, which is 0.05 to two decimals.
  2. 95% interval = 0.62 ± 2 × 0.05 = 0.52 to 0.72
  3. Sentence: with about 95% confidence, between 52% and 72% of all customers buy a coffee.

Connect

  • Test: the survey proportion, 0.62, is outside the range 0.40 to 0.60.
  • Estimate: the claim, 0.50, is outside the interval 0.52 to 0.72.
  • Compare: the distance between 0.62 and 0.50 is 0.12, and that is more than 2 SE = 0.10.
  • The test used 0.50 in the SE and the estimate used 0.62. Both standard errors are 0.05 after rounding, so the conclusion is the same.

Part 5 of 5 · questions 24 to 28

5Comparing surveys and stating conclusions

What you need to know

Two surveys can both be above the claim and still lead to different decisions:

  • It does not matter whether a survey is above or below the claim. When the claim is right, about half of all surveys are above it and about half are below it.
  • The decision depends on the distance in standard errors: more than 2 SE from the claim, or not.

The same survey mean can lead to different decisions when the sample sizes are different:

  • A larger sample has a smaller standard error.
  • The distance from the claim is then more standard errors, although the distance in dollars is the same.
  • A small difference from the claim is evidence against the claim only when the sample is large enough.

State your conclusions carefully:

  • A test does not give the chance that the claim is right.
  • A test tells you how often a survey such as yours would occur if the claim were right.
  • "Consistent with the claim" does not mean that the claim is proved. It means that the survey gives no reason to reject it.

Worked example: the same survey mean with different sample sizes

Three other stores each find a survey mean of $31.50 and a standard deviation of $10. The claim is $30. The stores surveyed 25, 100 and 400 customers.

  • The survey mean is the same in the three stores: $31.50, which is $1.50 above the claim.
  • A larger sample has a smaller standard error, so the same $1.50 is more standard errors.
  • With 400 customers, $31.50 is 3 SE from the claim. The survey is not consistent with the claim.
  • With 25 or with 100 customers, $31.50 is less than 2 SE from the claim. The survey is consistent with the claim. This does not prove the claim. The sample is too small to decide.

Optional reading · not on the exam

The same thinking in your own decisions

  • Nothing in this part is on the exam. There is nothing to calculate and nothing to memorize.
  • In parts 3 to 5 you tested a claim from a report. People use the same thinking whenever someone wants to change what is currently believed.
  • Each heading below is a question. Think about your own answer first, and then open it.
What do people believe before any data are collected?
  • In every test in this guide there was a claim, for example "customers spend $30 per visit". You assumed that the claim was right, and you asked whether the survey was consistent with it.
  • The belief that is accepted until there is evidence against it is called the null hypothesis. It is the established belief.
  • Nobody needs data to keep the null hypothesis. It is accepted by default.
  • People collect data when they want to find evidence against it.
  • The method in one sentence: we collect evidence to test whether we can reject the null hypothesis.
  • If the evidence is not strong enough, the null hypothesis is kept. This does not prove it. In parts 3 to 5 this was the decision "the survey is consistent with the claim".
Who has to bring the evidence?

The person who wants to reject the established belief has to bring the evidence. This is called the burden of proof.

SituationEstablished belief (null hypothesis)Who has to bring the evidence

A criminal trialThe accused is innocent.The prosecutor
A start-up asks an investor for moneyThis start-up will fail, as most start-ups do.The founder
A person applies for a bank loanThe applicant may not be able to repay.The applicant
A team proposes to replace the current supplierThe current supplier is good enough.The team
  • The null hypothesis depends on the situation. In a criminal trial the accused does not have to prove anything. In a loan application the applicant has to prove that they can repay.
  • A question to think about: the founder has already built and sold two companies and has a degree from Stanford or Harvard. Does the investor now start from a different belief? Or is the belief the same, and the founder's record is strong evidence against it? Both descriptions are reasonable. In both, the investor needs less new evidence from this founder than from a first-time founder.
  • Think of a decision in your own work. What is the established belief, and who would have to bring the evidence to change it?
Which mistake would you regret more?

You are deciding whether to pursue another degree. You do not know what the job market will be when you finish. There are two actions and two possible futures.

  • You can be wrong in two ways, and you cannot make both errors rare with the same data. If you ask for stronger evidence before you reject a belief, you reject true beliefs less often and you keep false beliefs more often.
  • You therefore have to decide which error is worse for you, and how often you are willing to make it.
  • For the degree: which of the two errors would you regret more?
  • Statistics books call the two errors the Type I error (rejecting a true belief) and the Type II error (keeping a false belief).
Why is the rule 5%, and not 20%?
  • The rule in this guide was 2 standard errors. With that rule, if the established belief is in fact true, you reject it wrongly only 5% of the time.
  • In business conversation people say this as "I am 95% confident". The note at the end of this page gives the exact meaning.
  • The rule is strict because a wrong rejection of an established belief is costly. Other people have made plans that depend on the belief. The person who rejected it, and was wrong, loses money or reputation.
  • The percentage is a choice. It depends on how strongly the belief is held and on the cost of rejecting it wrongly. 5% is usual in business questions. 1% or less is used when a wrong rejection would be very costly.
  • Courts in the United States make the same choice in words. A criminal conviction requires proof "beyond a reasonable doubt", because convicting an innocent person is very costly. A civil case requires only that the claim is "more likely than not".
  • Think of one belief in your company that you would reject only with very strong evidence, and one that you would reject with little evidence. What is the difference between them?
Is it unfair that a new idea needs proof and an old idea needs none?
  • It can seem unfair. A new idea needs strong evidence, and the established idea is kept without any.
  • Stability is useful. If a little evidence were enough to reject any belief, a company would change its strategy after every bad week, and nobody could plan.
  • No belief should be protected from questions either. Anyone may challenge an established belief.
  • Evidence allows both: the belief stays until someone brings enough data against it.
  • There is a price. With a stricter rule, a wrong belief is kept for longer.
Two people see the same data. Why do they still disagree?

A store manager is evaluated on customer satisfaction. The target is that at least 80% of customers are happy. A survey of 400 customers finds that 79% are happy.

  • For 400 customers, SE = √( 0.80 × 0.20 / 400 ) = 0.02, so 2 SE is 4 percentage points.
  • The null hypothesis is the opposite of what the person who brings the data wants to show.

  • Neither side is unreasonable. With 400 customers, 79% is too close to 80% to decide.
  • This happens often. Real data are noisy, so two people who start from different beliefs can see the same data and both keep their beliefs.
  • If each side could choose its own favorite belief as the null hypothesis, neither would ever have to change. For this reason the person who brings the data takes the opposite of what they want to show as the null hypothesis.
What does it take to change a belief?

More data. With more customers the standard error is smaller, and a small difference becomes enough evidence.

  • In daily life the data are usually few and noisy. Whichever belief you start from, you will usually keep it.
  • Beliefs change after overwhelming evidence: a very large difference, or a very large amount of data.
  • People also tend to stop collecting data as soon as the data support what they already believe.
  • Two habits help. Decide beforehand how much data you will collect. Decide beforehand which result would change your mind.
  • If no result would change your mind, you are not using the data to decide.

A note on wording. "95% confident" is used in this part in the way that it is used in business conversation. The exact statement is: if the established belief is in fact true, this rule makes you reject it wrongly only 5% of the time. It is not the chance that your decision is right, and it is not the chance that the belief is true. In the store manager example each side looks in one direction only, so a true null hypothesis is rejected wrongly no more than 5% of the time.

Technical discussion: the exact percentage in a test

  • This discussion is not on the exam. On the exam you compare the distance with 2 standard errors, and you write "fewer than 5%" or "more than 5%".
  • Statistics books and software report an exact percentage in place of "fewer than 5%". Read this discussion if you want to know how that percentage is calculated and how a decision is made from it.
  • The examples are the bookstores of part 3. The head office claims that customers spend $30 per visit on average. Each store surveys 100 customers and finds a standard deviation of $10, so SE = $1.
  • The first store found a survey mean of $33 and the second store found $31.50. A third store is added here. Its survey mean is $32.17.
What are the two ways to test a claim?

First way: start from the survey.

  • Calculate the 95% interval: survey mean ± 2 SE.
  • Check whether the claimed μ is inside the interval.
  • If the claimed μ is outside the interval, reject the claim.

Second way: start from the claim.

  • Assume that the claim is right. Then the mean of all possible sample means is the claimed μ, and their standard deviation is the SE. Part 1 showed both facts with the café of 8 customers.
  • Calculate how many SE the survey mean is from the claimed μ.
  • From that number, calculate how often a sample mean would be that far from μ, or farther.
  • If that would happen in fewer than 5% of samples, reject the claim.

How often would a sample mean be this far from the claim?
  • Sample means are approximately normal, so the percentage depends only on the distance in SE.
  • "This far" counts both directions: sample means this far above the claimed μ, and sample means this far below it.

The percentage is 3%. What does it mean, and what do you decide?

The third store found a survey mean of $32.17. That is 2.17 SE from the claim of $30, and the percentage is 3%.

  • If the claim is right, only 3% of all possible samples have a mean this far from $30, or farther.
  • Suppose you say: "This sample does not come from a population with a mean of $30." If the claim is in fact right, you are wrong. You would be wrong for 3% of all possible samples. These are the samples with means this far from $30.
  • People say this as: "We reject the claim with 97% confidence."
  • The requirement is 95% confidence. It allows a wrong rejection for 5% of samples. 3% is less than 5%.
  • Decision: reject the claim. In other words, reject the null hypothesis.

The rule for any test:

  • The percentage is less than 5%: reject the claim.
  • The percentage is more than 5%: keep the claim. This does not prove the claim.
  • 5% is a choice. The question "Why is the rule 5%, and not 20%?" on this page explains it. If the requirement is 99% confidence, the percentage has to be less than 1%.

The first way agrees, whatever the confidence:

  • An interval with more confidence is wider. The 95% interval is the survey mean ± 2 SE, the 97% interval is ± 2.17 SE, and the 99% interval is ± 2.58 SE.
  • Third store, 95% interval: 32.17 ± 2 × 1 = $30.17 to $34.17. The claim, $30, is outside it, so the claim is rejected.
  • Third store, 97% interval: 32.17 ± 2.17 × 1 = $30.00 to $34.34. The claim is exactly at the end of it.
  • Third store, 99% interval: 32.17 ± 2.58 × 1 = $29.59 to $34.75. The claim is inside it, so the claim is kept.
  • "Reject with 97% confidence" therefore has an exact meaning: the claim is outside every interval that has less than 97% confidence.

What the 3% is not. The 3% is not the chance that the claim is right, and 97% is not the chance that your decision is right. The 3% is calculated on the assumption that the claim is right. It is the share of samples that would be this far from the claim. Question 27 in part 5 asks you to correct this mistake.
